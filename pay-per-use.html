The problem space

The package model made our best sellers pay to guess, and they were leaving because of it

OLX's biggest sellers – car dealers, parts traders and seasonal listers – were leaving the platform, and the pace was accelerating month after month.

What they wanted was simple: value for money and no unnecessary hassle. The existing package model delivered neither.

It forced them to predict how many ads they'd need the following month and pay upfront for a fixed number that expired after roughly 30 days. Get the estimate wrong, and they lost either way.

But there was a third, less obvious problem.

Guess too high

Money wasted

A seller who bought 100 ads but only posted 70 lost the other 30 when the month ended. They paid for ads they never got to use.

Guess too low

Lost sales or pricier top-ups

Running out mid-month left two bad options: hold the inventory back and miss sales, or buy another package at a worse rate than the first one.

The hidden problem

Sellers capped themselves

Sellers with inventory ready to go would simply stop posting once their package ran out, because buying more felt like overspending. Nobody designed a limit, but the package worked like one.

Process at a glance

Two sections instead of four: from understanding the problem to proving the solution

The project followed the familiar path from identifying the problem to defining a direction, building a solution and validating it. But dividing a lean, four-month project into four separate phases would make the process look more rigid and structured than it really was.

In reality, the work came down to two connected parts: understanding the problem and making key decisions, then designing, building and validating the solution. AI supported both parts, but in different ways.

The problem and the direction

Four months to deliver, so this stayed lean: confirm the problem, then sharpen it

The question: are we solving the right problem?

With only four months to deliver, we couldn't afford a long research phase, and we didn't need one. The archived seller research already told us we were solving the right problem. So discovery focused on two things: benchmarking how other platforms handle pay-per-use pricing, and building a hypothesis grounded in the research and data we already had. Then we moved into ideation as fast as we could.

Analyse the existing research archive
We loaded the existing seller interview archive (Poland, Romania) into NotebookLM and analysed it with Toqan (OLX's internal AI tool), to double-check we were solving the right problem before spending on new research. The rules were strict: every claim cited to a transcript, open questions marked as open, nothing made up. The top 3 churn drivers turned out to be Costs/ROI (49%), competitor pricing (30%), and platform usability (10–16%).

What the earlier research already told us
Benchmarking

How Allegro and eBay charge sellers

Both competitors work on store subscriptions plus per-listing fees. Neither asks sellers to guess an ad count upfront. That set the bar for what migrating sellers would consider normal.

Tested and rejected

The hybrid model had already failed

An earlier project tested a hybrid model (listing fee plus sales commission) in three hypotheses. In-depth interviews assessed all three as not true: sellers saw the hybrid as unfair. And with most car parts sold offline, there's no reliable event to charge a commission on.

Category economics

Car parts sell slowly

Only 14% of active ads had been posted in the last 28 days. The average ad was 421 days old, and 75% of listings stayed active for 76 days or more. Slow-moving inventory fits 30-day ad packages especially badly.

Self-imposed posting cap
Sellers stop posting when packages run out because spending more feels like a loss. PPU has to make unlimited posting feel safe as well as fair.
Vertical user muscle memory
Sellers coming over from Otomoto and Autovit already expect pay-per-use, it's just how those platforms work. If OLX's version feels unfamiliar or overcomplicated, we risk losing them right in the migration window, and with it the revenue they bring. The safer move is to keep the model close to what they already know, not reinvent it.
Team decision

The AI tools found plenty of patterns in the archive and the earlier research. The team decided which ones set the direction: with hybrid commission models already tested and rejected as unfair, and no reliable way to charge on offline sales, we focused the project on pure usage-based pricing. Charge for ads actually posted, nothing else.

Two personas and a problem statement, built to move fast

The question: how do we turn a known problem into something the team can act on?

The opportunity brief had already named the problem, so this stayed lean. I built two personas, a high-volume lister and a mid-volume lister, straight from the NotebookLM research we'd already analysed, and used them to sharpen the opportunity brief into a problem statement and a set of HMWs the team could design against.

Problem statement

High-volume postpaid sellers need a monetisation model that accommodates unpredictable inventory volumes without financial waste or operational friction, because the current package model forces them to predict and commit to the unpredictable.

HMW questions: 5 that drove Develop
HMW let sellers post freely without committing to volume upfront?
HMW make bracket pricing transparent before the first ad is posted?
HMW make sellers want to post more to unlock cheaper rates?
HMW show accumulated costs clearly enough to build trust before month-end?
HMW feel familiar to a seller migrating from Otomoto on day one?
Team decision

Claude drafted 5 versions of the problem statement. we chose the one that named the guessing itself as the problem. That version steered the team away from "let's build better packages" and toward getting rid of the forecast altogether.

Building and proving it

A 4-hour engineering ideation session that produced 7 working concepts, voted and stress-tested

The question: what could the new model look like, and which idea is worth testing?

Instead of a traditional design sprint, we ran a workshop in Lovable with engineers building alongside designers, then a second ideation round with product, commercial, and engineering stakeholders. 7 interactive prototypes were made and voted on in a single session. After the workshops, the designs went through several design critiques with the design team and engineering.

What went into the workshop. Everything from the problem and direction work so far, condensed onto one board: the problem, the current user journey, the supporting data, the hypothesis (if we offer pay-per-use, then friction and complaints drop and inventory grows, because it removes the upfront commitment), and the success metrics: stable or growing ARPU and net new listings, plus UMUX-Lite above 70. Nobody ideated blind.
How the team stayed aligned

A weekly rhythm, born out of that first workshop

After the ideation session, I started sitting down every week with product, commercial, and engineering. That's where product decisions got made, designs got reviewed, and go-to-market plans, engineering updates, and the roadmap got discussed, along with any blockers and concerns, all the way through to delivery.

Concept selected

Postpaid PPU with volume discounts + benefit tiers

Sellers choose a Standard, Premium, or Ultra tier (listing quality). Post unlimited ads. Billed at month-end, with lower rates as volume grows. Showing sellers how close they are to a cheaper rate turns transparent billing into a reason to post more. No expiry, no upfront commitment.

Before: the monthly reset. Rico buys a package, runs out or lets ads expire, and starts over every 30 days. 10% of ads in Parts packages went unused, money sellers paid for nothing.
After: set it once, keep running. The same journey, redesigned. Rico picks a flexible package once, posts as needed with bracket pricing applied automatically, and gets one monthly invoice for what he actually used.
Team decision

Dot voting showed the favourites, and then we made the final pick together as a team, with everyone agreeing: volume discounts with benefit tiers. Showing sellers how close they are to a cheaper rate gives them a reason to post more, which answered the "sellers cap themselves" insight from earlier directly.

⚠ The pivot: volume discounts to incremental brackets

The team's first instinct was a simple volume discount: post more, get a lower rate on everything. Together with the product manager, engineering and stakeholders, we decided to walk that back. It had technical obstacles that would have slowed delivery, and a bigger problem: it was easy to game, a seller could inflate volume just to unlock a lower rate, then coast, which put real revenue at risk. Incremental bracket pricing (a fixed rate per tier of ads, not one rate applied retroactively to everything) closed that loophole while keeping the part sellers actually wanted: a visible reason to post more.

Tested with 30 PL sellers: UMUX-Lite 85 (Excellent), 4 issues prioritised for iteration

The question: does it actually work for real sellers?

In June 2026 we ran an unmoderated Maze study with 30 Polish sellers from the Car Parts, Vehicles for Parts, and Agriculture Parts categories: 3 tasks, 20 test blocks, scored with UMUX-Lite. Sellers tested against a fully working prototype, built in Claude Code and published as a public website, so what they clicked through felt like the real product rather than a mockup. What we found went straight into the final design. The sessions were with Polish-speaking sellers, so the tested prototype was in Polish and showed prices in PLN, which is why the findings below quote PLN; the prototype screens elsewhere on this page show €.

Team decision

4 issues ranked fix-before-handoff

UMUX-Lite gave us the overall score, but deciding what to fix was a team call. Out of everything the 30 sessions turned up, we marked 4 issues as must-fix before handoff. Each one became a real design change: a tooltip explaining the confusing 200 PLN charge, the price per listing shown right away instead of hidden in a dropdown, a label explaining why Agriculture sits under "Parts", and simpler, colour-coded pricing screens. The test shaped the final design instead of just approving it.

Handoff, code-to-design

The prototype was the spec

I designed in code from the start, so there was never a separate mockup to keep in sync. Every screen from Claude Code went into Figma through MCP, which kept it consistent with our design system, the way we're required to document every project. Engineering didn't need a written interaction spec: they built from the same working prototype sellers had just tested.

Iteration & what's next

Tested, improved, and heading into a real-world pilot

All 4 issues from testing were fixed in the final design before handoff. The model isn't live yet. We're rolling it out in steps, each with its own way of collecting feedback, because the revenue from migrating sellers is riding on getting it right.

Done · Usability testing

UMUX-Lite 85 · Excellent

30 Polish sellers tested it. 93–100% completed the core tasks, before a single sprint of development. Handoff is complete: the prototype is documented in Figma, and engineering is building from what sellers actually used.

Next · Pilot

50 real sellers on the live model

50 sellers will use pay-per-use with real inventory and real bills. The sales team collects their feedback directly. That's the kind of signal a usability test can't give you.

Then · Full rollout

Checking everyone understands it

When pay-per-use reaches all 5,882 sellers, an in-product survey will check whether brackets, tiers, and billing actually make sense to people. That closes the loop on the transparency goal we set early on.

What we'll measure

The pilot and rollout test the hypothesis directly. Retention tells us whether sellers stay, inventory whether they post more once the upfront commitment is gone, and ARPU whether the model still pays off.

Main metric

Seller retention

How many postpaid sellers stay on the platform and keep listing after moving to pay-per-use. Cost was the #1 reason sellers left (49%), so this is the clearest test of whether removing the upfront commitment works, and it protects the migration revenue.

Secondary metric

Inventory increase

Growth in active listings from high-volume sellers, who no longer need to hold stock back when a package runs out. It shows whether the self-imposed posting cap is gone, and whether we can turn around the −24.4% year-on-year decline.

Additional metric

ARPU

Average revenue per seller. Pay-per-use charges for what sellers actually post, so this shows whether more listings from retained sellers add up to healthy revenue, or whether the model gives away too much compared with packages.

Honest reflection

What the AI workflow changed, and what it didn't

AI didn't replace the design thinking. It took away the busywork around it, so more of my time went to the decisions only a designer can make.

Where AI actually helped

Claude turned research and data I already had into a usable hypothesis fast, so I could spend the saved time on benchmarking and framing instead of formatting
Recipes turned repeatable tasks (archive analysis, insight extraction, synthesis) into fast, consistent outputs rather than blank-slate efforts each time
Designing straight in Claude Code and pushing it into Figma through MCP meant the design system stayed documented without me redrawing anything after the fact
Lovable brought engineers into ideation as builders rather than reviewers, so constraints surfaced in hours instead of sprint cycles
Engineering built from the same working prototype sellers had just tested, so there was no separate interaction spec to keep in sync or lose fidelity to

What I owned as the designer

Helping the team decide which insights actually mattered. The agents find patterns; the team decides which ones can carry a project (see the "Team decision" blocks in each phase)
Keeping product, commercial, legal, and engineering aligned. No agent does that
Keeping the AI research grounded in the real interviews, not in assumptions
